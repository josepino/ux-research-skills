# JTBD switch-timeline interview (why someone switched)

**When to read it:** the team wants to understand why a person chose, left or switched a product (adoption, churn, competition). For general discovery, stay with the normal guide in `guide-template.md`.

**Attribution and confidence.** The "switch timeline" interview method is associated with Bob Moesta and with the Jobs to Be Done theory of Clayton Christensen and colleagues. This summary was built from secondary sources (descriptions by Great Question and similar), not from the authors' original texts: treat it as an adaptation and check it against the original material before teaching it as "the method".

## The core idea
A purchase or a switch is not an instant: it is a story with phases. Instead of asking what the person wants, you reconstruct **what happened** from when something started to bother them until they used the solution. This respects the golden rule of the guide: past behavior, concrete episode.

## Phases and probes

| Phase | Goal | Opening question | Probes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. First thought | Find the moment the idea of switching appeared | "Think back to the first moment you thought something had to change. What was going on?" | Where were you? Who else was there? What had you been doing until then? What triggered it that day and not another? |
| 2. Passive looking | See what they noticed without actively searching | "After that, what did you start noticing?" | What ads, comments or recommendations did you register? What options showed up without you looking? |
| 3. Active looking | Understand what they compared and how | "When did you start looking seriously? Tell me how that went." | What did you look at? Who did you talk to? What did you rule out and why? What did you still need to know? |
| 4. Decision | Rebuild the final stretch | "Tell me about the day you decided." | What almost stopped you? What made you take the step? Did anything change at the last minute? |
| 5. First use | See expectation against reality | "What was the first time you used it like?" | What did you expect? What surprised you? What did you do differently from what you thought? Did you keep using the previous one? |

Order: let them tell the story in its own sequence and use the probes to fill gaps; do not read the table like a questionnaire. Ask for approximate dates and concrete objects (which screen, which message, which price) to anchor memory.

## The four forces: what to listen for
While listening, note each cue under one of these forces (the decision to switch is explained by how they balance):

| Force | Direction | Typical language | Probe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Push (of the current situation) | Toward the change | frustration, "I couldn't take it anymore", an event that broke the routine | "What didn't work for you about what you were using?" |
| Pull (of the new solution) | Toward the change | aspiration, "I imagined that...", a concrete promise | "What did you imagine would be different?" |
| Anxiety (about the new) | Against the change | doubts, fear of losing data, of making mistakes, of cost | "What worried you about switching?" |
| Habit (of the current) | Against the change | "I've always done it this way", comfort, cost of learning | "What was comfortable about what you already had?" |

Signs that depth is missing: there is only pull ("it's better") without push; nobody mentions anxiety or habit; the story jumps from the idea to the purchase with no searching.

## Adapt by participant type

| Type | Focus | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| New customer (switched recently) | Fresh memory: all phases | Full timeline; emphasis on phases 3-5 |
| Someone who left | What pushed the exit and what replaced it | Run it as an exit interview: start from the moment of leaving, go back to phases 1-2 and ask what they use now; take care not to blame |
| Long-term current customer | What made them stay and what tempted them to leave | Pick an episode of real switching or doubt; if they bought long ago, memory is weak: anchor on the last time they reconsidered |
| Person who has not switched yet | An incomplete story | Tell only as far as they got; avoid "would you buy it?", which is hypothetical |

## Cautions
- Reconstructing a story also distorts (post-hoc rationalization): treat what is said as narrative, not proven cause, and look for patterns across participants.
- It is not a usability method and does not evaluate screens: for that, `ux-usability-testing`.
- To analyze: separate observation / quote / interpretation and mark the force in the grid (`analysis-grid.md`); cross-participant synthesis is `ux-synthesis`.
