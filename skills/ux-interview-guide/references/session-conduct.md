# Session conduct, logistics and debrief

Read it when preparing the session or coaching someone who will interview for the first time.

## 1. Before the session
- Test audio, recording and links; have the guide and the participant's details at hand (why they are here, what they use today). Arriving disorganized eats time and erodes trust (UserBit).
- Give little advance information and do not reveal what you hope to hear: over-informing "primes" the participant (UserBit).
- Rehearse with a colleague or a real user (pilot): it reveals confusing questions, unnatural order and timings. You may adjust the guide during the study, but do not change the objectives (NN/g).
- Send reminders a few days before; state the incentive and how it is paid.
- Avoid group sessions for this purpose: focus groups tend to reflect whoever talks the most (Stéphanie Walter, opinion).

## 2. Spoken introduction (5 min)
Introduce yourself (and the team), explain the general goal without biasing, the duration, what kind of questions to expect and how what is said will be used. Make clear there are no right answers and that they can pause or skip. Confirm verbally the permission to record and to share internally even if you already have it in writing (GitLab). Trick: say aloud "I confirm the recording has started and I have your permission" while recording, so you verify it is really recording (Lyssna). Speak slowly: it calms the participant (NN/g).

## 3. Silence, listening and body language
- **Intentional silence:** in the US silence becomes uncomfortable after 4 seconds (Koudenburg et al., cited by NN/g); a practical rule is to count to 7 before speaking. Do not finish the participant's sentence: they tend to say "yes" and drop their idea. Wait a few seconds even when they seem done: they often add something, sometimes contradictory. Careful: in a usability test, too long a silence with someone who is lost frustrates them; total silence is often a sign of a nervous moderator. Tolerance for silence varies by culture.
- **Active listening:** paraphrase, summarize, name emotions, avoid judging. Neutral reactions ("I see", "mm"), echo what was said.
- **Rapport is not friendship:** the participant does not need to like you to trust you; it is enough that they feel heard (NN/g). Start with easy questions and avoid ones that feel like an exam (for example "what was the last book you read?").
- **Body and voice (Dscout):** sit at the same height (beside rather than opposite), compact posture, slight forward lean, nod and use micro-expressions (important remotely, where the body is barely visible), do not stare without blinking, soft conversational voice, put your phone away and say so if you will take notes.
- **Participant non-verbal signals (Gramcko, UX Collective, 2020; summary):** look at clusters of signals, not a single one. Crossed arms or legs, averted gaze, tense smile, raised or furrowed eyebrows, pressed lips or sighs, a hand on the face and leaning back may indicate discomfort, doubt, confusion or frustration, but also tiredness or concentration. They are hypotheses, not verdicts, and vary by person and culture. What to do: probe ("what did you mean by that?", "what are you trying to do?", "is there anything else you want to add?"), re-read their earlier answer and ask them to say more, and reassure them that honesty is welcome. Note the signal and the task/question where it happened; if it repeats in a flow, prioritize that point. Being present lets you probe in the moment; video lets you review it. Your own signals are contagious (mirroring): watch them.
- **Setting:** if you are in the participant's space, they are the host; in yours, you are.
- **Avoid the "tribunal":** with two interviewers, do not make it feel like a job interview.

## 4. Common mistakes (UserBit, Wudpecker, Maze, Marc Abraham)
Going unprepared · recording without permission · letting outside observers watch live (Hawthorne effect: the participant feels evaluated and softens criticism; better to limit observers, brief them and debrief separately) · priming · talking too much · asking too many questions (better few topics in depth) · avoiding or ignoring negative criticism · assuming the meaning of a vague answer · using the interview to sell or validate a solution · rushing from one interview to the next without reflecting.

## 5. Notes, recording and observers
- Ideal format: interviewer + note-taker + participant (NN/g, IxDF, Walter). The note-taker uses the guide as structure; the interviewer may take minimal notes of their own.
- Observers: they should not speak. If possible, have them watch the recording. A private chat channel (for example Slack) lets them suggest questions without interrupting; close by asking if they have more (Lyssna).
- Recording frees you from writing everything and lets you go back; always ask for explicit consent.

## 6. Duration and number of interviews
- Discovery: 40-60 min; continuous interviews shorter: 20-30 min (H. Omitogun in Maze). Typical guide range: 30-60 min. Time matters more than number of questions: 4 deep questions can yield more than 20 quick ones.
- How many: sources disagree. Eleken cites 5 users (an NN/g recommendation originally meant for usability tests) with a possible range of 1-30; Looppanel and User Interviews talk about 5-10 per segment; User Interviews suggests 5-10 working parents in its 30-60 min example. Decide by diversity of segments and saturation, not by a fixed number.
- Useful distinction (Walter): contextual = observe/interview in the real environment and ask them to do tasks; generative = structured or semi-structured one-to-one.

## 7. Debrief and quick synthesis (LogRocket / Bart Krawczyk)
Between interviews, do not jump to the next one without closing the previous:
1. **One-page summary ("snapshot")**: name/photo, key facts, insights, opportunities, one memorable quote, admin data (date, interviewer, links). Nobody will watch hours of video.
2. **Go deeper as a team (~10 min)**: how does it compare with previous interviews? Is there a separate segment? Does anything contradict our beliefs and how do we validate it?
3. **Update artifacts** (maps, personas, repository) and adjust the guide for the next one.
It helps retain, align the team and reduce individual bias. For the full flow: `ux-research-ops` and `ux-synthesis`.
