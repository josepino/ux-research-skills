# Catalog of biases and how to mitigate them

Read this file when you must diagnose bias in a guide, survey, session or report, or prepare a self-awareness exercise. All examples are paraphrases of Maze and User Interviews.

## 1. Reduce, not eliminate
Biases are useful mental shortcuts; they are a problem when you do not notice them. Bias is especially strong in qualitative work (more subjective) and in democratized research (PMs, marketing or design without training): there, someone with a trained eye should supervise plan, analysis and synthesis (User Interviews).

## 2. Researcher biases
| Bias | What it looks like | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation | You highlight what supports your favorite and sweep away what does not (for example an even preference between two apps, but the report stresses only one) | Record counter-evidence, report ties, ask for cross-review |
| Framing | "I see option A worked better for you, tell me" presupposes a preference | Neutral questions; present findings without loaded narrative |
| Anchoring | "Would you pay 9.99?" fixes the mental price | Ask open or ranges; do not reveal a number first |
| Serial position (primacy/recency) | You remember the start and end better; lists of 30 options concentrate choices at the extremes | Record, take notes, randomize order, short lists |
| Peak-end | One intense emotional moment or the closing dominates memory | Review the whole session, not memory |
| Clustering illusion | You see a "pattern" in random data or nearby anecdotes (example: 0.5% citing price looked like a trend; it was about 10 people a year) | Count frequencies, size against the total base |
| Empathy gap | Your emotional state (a bad meeting) ruins the interview without you noticing | Pause before sessions; do not schedule after tense events |
| False consensus | "It happens to me, so to everyone" | Validate with data and with users, not only the team |
| Sunk cost | You continue because you already invested (related: commitment and loss aversion) | Clear goals from the start, data-based decision points, decision matrix (The Decision Lab) |
| Implicit/cultural/racial | Stereotypes; interpreting with your own cultural norms | List assumptions; the other's perspective; local help |
| Hindsight | "It was obvious" | Record predictions before the study |
| Survivorship | You look only at those who succeeded | Include dropouts and non-users |
| Fundamental attribution | You blame the person, not the context | Review situational factors (did the recording or the observer fail?) |
| Transparency | You think your ideas are understood on their own | Make explicit and verify |

## 3. Participant and instrument biases
- **Social desirability**: they answer what they think pleases you or hide what is shameful (partners, crimes). Mitigate: ask about concrete past behavior, normalize, anonymity, observational method.
- **Question order**: one question primes the next (example: "your favorite Disney movie?" before "your favorite movie?"). Mitigate: general to specific, and randomize.
- **Leading / loaded / double-barreled questions**: use neutral, simple language without jargon and one topic per question; offer the full scale. Neutral examples from Maze: "what challenges or positive aspects did you find?"
- **Fraudulent or ill-fitting participants** bias the study (User Interviews).
- **Reusing the same participants** familiarizes them with your style and changes their behavior.
- **Incentive**: see consent-and-data.md section 4.
- **Sampling**: only the willing/connected arrive -> several channels and quotas.

## 4. Signs of bias in others
A repeated anecdote without data; assumptions presented as facts; only the answers that support one position are quoted; criticism always extreme toward one person; blaming individuals instead of context; repeating methods with the same participants.

## 5. Strategies (User Interviews)
Know the bias types · self-awareness (introspection, taking your time, the other's perspective) · detect others' bias tactfully · state assumptions, goals and hypotheses in the plan · one or two "critical friends" who review topic, method, sample, timing, questions and analysis · mixed methods · representative participants · pause before every session · no leading questions · poker face · invite other teams to observe and take their own notes · write down facts, not opinions.
Useful short quote (Erika Hall, cited by User Interviews): surveys are dangerous because they are so easy to make.

## 6. 5W1H assumptions exercise
Before the study, write what you believe about: who the user is, what problem the product solves, when they use it, where (car, phone, desktop), how (app, web, physical). Each assumption becomes a hypothesis to refute.

## 7. Common reporting mistakes
- Quotes without a count: say how many participants said or did the same (all, most, a handful, one) and do not leave quotes alone (IxDF).
- Choosing examples that fit what you expected.
- Mixing your interpretations with what the participant said.
- Trusting a small percentage without its base.

## 8. Group sessions, claims to verify and consistency across interviews
Contributions from two popular articles (secondary summaries; see `sources.md`).
- **Bandwagon effect in groups**: in group interviews or exercises people align with the dominant opinion and the dissenter goes quiet; whoever answers last is conditioned by the previous ones. Mitigate: ask each participant to write or record their opinion *before* discussing as a group.
- **Enthusiastic claims ("yes, all the time!")**: do not take them as evidence. Verify in several ways: why they do it (did they have no other option or do they like it?), how many times last week, whether they can show it, whether they might want to please you. Playing devil's advocate against your own hypothesis throughout the study is the cheapest mitigation of confirmation bias.
- **Hindsight in accounts of the past**: when asking reasons for something that already happened, the person may invent plausible causes without basis. Ask for concrete episodes and check facts; if you do not know, admit it instead of filling in with a false explanation.
- **Social desirability, nuance**: besides mitigating it (past behavior, anonymity), you can reframe the question toward a third party ("what would you improve if you designed it for your mom?") so usability criticism does not clash with the image the person wants to project. This technique comes from a single article: test it in a pilot.
- **Framing with adjectives**: "do you like the *improved* button design or not?" pushes toward the positive; prefer "tell me how the button layout works for you". Asking "what did you like / dislike" fixes attention on poles and can color the rest of the session; neutral alternatives: "tell me about the last time you used it", "how do you feel using it?".
- **Serial position and primacy/recency across interview rounds**: with 5-10 or more interviews in a row before synthesizing, the first and last weigh too much; in card sorting the middle of a long list is ignored. Mitigate with notes/recording per session and, as another source argues, interview everyone the same way so you can compare and notice atypical moments.
- **Sunk cost in the study**: divide into stages with explicit continue-or-stop decisions (the small-experiments approach of Lean Startup) and prioritize work that benefits the people who use the product.
- **Empathy map** as support against framing: it forces you to record also what the person sees and hears, not only what they say and do.
- Contrast with what is already written: none of this contradicts sections 2-5; both sources agree that biases are reduced, not eliminated.
