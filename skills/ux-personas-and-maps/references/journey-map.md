## 1. Family of maps: which to use
| | Empathy | Journey (customer/user) | Experience | Service blueprint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | The user's mindset | One actor achieving a goal with **one product/service** | A generic person achieving a goal, **independent of the product** | The people, evidence and processes of the organization behind each touchpoint |
| Chronological | No | Yes | Yes | Yes and hierarchical |
| Structure | 4 quadrants | 4 lanes: phases, actions, thoughts, mindset/emotions | The same 4 lanes | Customer actions, frontstage, backstage, support processes |
| Relation | 1 map per persona | 1 map per persona | Persona-agnostic | Part 2 of the journey |
| Use when | At the start, to categorize interview notes | Any time, as a shared reference | Before the journey, or to converge several experiences | After the journey; before organizational changes; omnichannel or multi-area experiences |

An experience map is "the parent" of the journey, and the blueprint "the grandchild". Example: an experience map of how people get around a city (walking, bike, taxi...) -> pains emerge from it (unknown fare, weather, payment) -> a future journey of how one type of user orders a car with an app -> a blueprint of what the company does internally (assign driver, calculate fare).

Double benefits of any map: (1) the **process** of building it forces a conversation and an aligned mental model in the team; (2) the **artifact** serves to communicate and as a basis for decisions. No method alone "makes or breaks" a project; the ideal is to combine them.

## 2. Three prior decisions
Before starting, decide and write it in the map header.

1. **Current (as-is) vs future (to-be).**
   - *Current*: documents existing problems and pains; to persuade (frequency and magnitude of pains, with thoughts and emotions as narrative), align a team on a validated problem and as a basis for more research (show it to customers and ask them to react). Highlight the good too.
   - *Future*: the best case for an existing or nonexistent product; it serves as a shared "north", to derive experience principles and to prioritize features/content/service points. One author describes it as less tied to objective data and more open to the customer's wishes and hopes.
2. **Hypothesis (assumption-first) vs research (research-first).**
   - *Hypothesis*: a 1 to 3 day workshop with a multidisciplinary team, using existing knowledge. Advantages: it educates and builds buy-in, lowers the entry barrier, is fast. **Main risk: the process stops before validation and a critical decision is made with a hypothesis map.** Assess first how good what you already know is: how much, how solid the methodology, how recent, how broad and how deep. The more positive answers, the lower the risk.
   - *Research*: prior primary research, more rigor, but long and expensive and with less chance to involve the team; include frequent progress readouts so you do not "disappear". Works when internal anecdotes carry no weight in the organization or the scope is small and under your authority.
3. **Low vs high fidelity.** Low: sticky notes or a collaborative spreadsheet, little commitment, ideal for early stages. High: digital and polished, ideal for circulating among many people; more readable but less flexible.

**NN/g hybrid recommendation** (for most teams): first a current hypothesis map with stakeholders -> research (interviews, diaries, or invite customers to the workshop to react to the draft) -> version 2 with primary data -> once pains are well understood, visioning for the future map.

## 3. Components of a journey map (NN/g)
Five common elements, regardless of visual look:
1. **Actor**: the person/user living the journey; one point of view per map (if the university wants to see student and teacher, those are two maps). Aligned with a persona and with data-based actions.
2. **Scenario + expectations**: the situation and the actor's goal/need (e.g. switching mobile plans to save money; expectation: finding all information easily). Can be real or anticipated. Journey maps pay off most in sequences of events, processes over time or multichannel experiences.
3. **Phases**: high-level stages that organize the rest.
4. **Actions, mindsets and emotions**: *actions* = a narrative of steps, not a log of micro-interactions; *mindsets* = thoughts, questions, motivations and information needs, ideally with **verbatim quotes** from the research; *emotions* = a **single line** across phases showing peaks and valleys (delight vs frustration).
5. **Opportunities** (+ owners and metrics): what to do with what was learned, who is responsible for each change, where the biggest opportunities are and how improvement will be measured.

Additional elements useful depending on the goal (IxDF/others): an explicit timeline (e.g. a week, from recognizing the problem to renewal), **channels** and **touchpoints**, insights and barriers to progress. Typical layout: persona and scenario on top; thoughts, actions and feelings in the middle; insights and barriers at the bottom. Some 2026 guides add AI touchpoints (chatbots, recommenders) and mark where automation helps and where it creates friction: use this if the product includes AI, without replacing human interpretation.

## 4. How to choose phases
- Phases are the columns; they are defined first and must cover **the whole experience** (including drop-offs). They have to be high-level: "search prices, read reviews, compare" are actions within an Explore phase, not three phases.
- Few phases so the map reads without horizontal scrolling.
- Examples from the source (guides, not mandatory): e-commerce: discover, try, buy, use, seek support. Big purchase: engage, inform, research, evaluate, justify. B2B: purchase, adoption, retention, expansion, advocacy. Travel booking: research and plan, book, before travel, during, after. Generic customer journey: awareness, consideration, purchase, retention, advocacy (a cycle, not an end).
- In an experience map, phases reflect changes in the person's goal/context, without assuming the product (buying a train ticket: discovering, buying, using, returning). One experience-map guide recommends naming stages with gerunds ("paying", "choosing") to reflect actions that extend over time.
- Prioritize patterns repeated across participants (a workaround many use, a doubt in every interview, a consistent peak of frustration). A single user's isolated request goes to another artifact.
- If a lane is hard to fill, research for that phase is missing.

## 5. Recommended process (synthesis of several guides)
1. Define the map's goal, who will use it and which experience it addresses. Assemble the team and scope.
2. Define actor and scenario (research-based when possible); make the 3 prior decisions.
3. Research touchpoints and channels: interviews, surveys, social listening, analytics, tickets, competitive intelligence.
4. Make an empathy map of the actor to pour out what they say, think, feel and do, and what bothers them.
5. Sketch the journey (touchpoints x timeline x emotions at each point).
6. Iterate and polish; share with stakeholders so they understand how it serves them.
7. Kerry Bodine's 5-step summary: hypothesis -> research to validate it -> map -> identify root causes (e.g. with the "5 whys", because many pains are systemic) -> design customer-centered solutions (many ideas, prototype and test with customers/employees/partners, iterate). Remember that the goal of the initiative is to change how the organization works, not to produce maps.

## 6. How to build an experience map (UX Magazine method)
- Research (if possible two people per interview: one leads, one takes notes) and pour into a spreadsheet what people **do, feel and think**, written in first person ("I think the quality is bad"); then onto sticky notes without the "I".
- Group behaviors and feelings by customer **goals** (there can be 40 to 80 goals depending on the product; e.g. "pay for an item"), and underneath: behaviors, positive and negative feelings. No customer will exhibit all the behaviors: the map represents everyone; the order of goals is a judgment based on the research. Then group goals into stages.
- **Beliefs** (what they think about the product) are grouped separately and go **outside** the map, so as not to break the flow and because they describe what they think, not how they use it.
- Add the business view (representatives of each area) and the competition. To see competitors, longitudinal studies/diaries (weeks or months) capture interactions invisible to the organization (e.g. asking a competitor for a price); desk research gives much less.
- Present the map by telling the stories heard in the research; this raises empathy and reveals gaps and strategic projects.

## 7. Workshops and validation with customers
- **Hypothesis map** (Tincher): teams of 5 or 6 people, each with a stretch of the journey: orthodoxies (internal beliefs about the company), a typical persona with name and data, steps/touchpoints (including unmeasurable ones, like asking a friend for advice), interactions, "I think vs I say" (the frustrated customer may be polite with the agent), emotions, **moments of truth** and internal processes/rules. There are moments the company never sees (e.g. logging in many times to a portal, if those who design it never have problems).
- Uses: guide what and how to ask in the research, and later compare with the customer-based map. When comparing, start with what the team got right before marking the deviations.
- Prudence: a hypothesis map **is not a customer journey map**; without talking to customers the insights are limited. Tincher recommends **not sharing it with customers**: if they only react, they contribute less of their own and the study scope is limited to the points on the map (a huge part of the journey happens outside the company). Note: NN/g proposes the opposite with the draft of the current map (show it to customers and ask them to react) and Bodine invites customers to workshops. See section 11.
- Bodine usually runs workshops with about 20 customers and a somewhat smaller number of employees; in some cases the pains the company was about to invest in fixing were of little importance to customers.
- Hypothetical example (Eleken): it may turn out that very few customers join a loyalty program; the "thoughts" lane in the reservation phase shows that creating the account takes too long. Thoughts explain the metrics.

## 8. From map to action
- Review phase by phase: where behavior, thought and emotion show the experience breaking. Turn each friction into a concrete **opportunity** with an owner and a metric.
- Friction almost never falls on a single team (a checkout abandonment can involve form, flow and load times across three teams); that is why it helps to have the owners in the room.
- For the future map of a nonexistent product: analyze 3 to 5 main competitors with the criteria of the map's phases: use their products doing your persona's tasks, read reviews (recurring complaints) and, if you can, run usability tests on them.
- Once a big opportunity is identified, take it down to a **user story map** to plan releases (the journey is discovery and understanding; the story map is planning and implementation).

## 9. Concepts that get confused
- **Journey map vs user flow**: the flow describes technical micro-interactions, usually in one stage, without feelings or touchpoints; the journey gives the big picture with emotions and pains.
- **Journey vs user story map**: see above.
- **User journey vs customer journey**: NN/g treats them as interchangeable ("aligning the content matters more than the name"). Other guides distinguish: user journey = interaction with a digital product (for UX/product); customer journey = the full relationship with the brand, online and offline (marketing, sales, support). Use your team's definition and state it in the header.
- **Buyer / user / customer journey** (IxDF): buyer = path to purchase; user = experience with the digital platform; customer = full lifecycle.
- A hypothesis map and a research-based one are not the same; mark it.

## 10. Common mistakes
- Mapping several actors in one map.
- Filling the actions lane with every click.
- Emotions without the cause; an emotion line disconnected from quotes.
- Deciding with an unvalidated hypothesis map.
- A map made once, with no owners or metrics.
- Fixing the symptom without looking for the organizational root cause.
- Stopping at the map: what produces value are the decisions and changes that follow.

## 11. Discrepancies between sources
- **Does the experience map include competitors and the business view?** NN/g defines it as product- and business-agnostic; UX Magazine and Eleken present it also showing what the business and competitors do. Decide and clarify the scope.
- **Journey = one persona?** NN/g and almost all sources: yes, one per map. An experience map, by contrast, generalizes across user types.
- **Show the draft to customers?** NN/g: yes, to spark discussion and refine; Tincher: not the hypothesis map, because it biases. Suggestion from this skill (not from the sources): interview first with open questions and show the map only afterwards, to validate and correct.
- **"User" vs "customer" journey**: see section 9.
