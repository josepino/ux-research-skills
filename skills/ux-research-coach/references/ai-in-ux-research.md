## 1. Golden rule
AI is very good with **language** data (interviews, open survey answers, diaries) and weak with **behavior** data (what the user does on screen). It is used more in planning and analysis, and less during the session. It works like an intern: it needs context, instructions, a template and human review (NN/g, "Accelerating Research with AI"). Why it matters: the researcher stays responsible for interpretation, prioritization and ethics.

## 2. Map by stage
| Stage | Delegate (draft) | Do not delegate / watch |
|---|---|---|
| Desk research | Explore a topic, vocabulary, what to search for | Using it as a fact-checker: it can invent sources. Ask for primary sources and open them |
| Planning | Ideas for objectives, methods, screener/survey/interview questions, test tasks | It often generates **leading** questions or tasks even when given best practices; human review is mandatory |
| Documentation | Plan, screener, consent form, script, starting from **your own template** | Letting it invent the structure: permissions/consent get lost. Review the legal part by hand |
| During the session | Backup notes in interviews (useful if you are a team of one) | Moderating or "observing" usability tests: it cannot see what the person does; it only reads the transcript |
| AI-run interviews | Structured feedback at scale, different languages, screening | Semi-structured interviews, complex or fuzzy topics: they do not build rapport or read expressions |
| Analysis | Transcribe, translate, summarize with timestamps, clean data/PII, first-pass codes and clusters (see `ux-transcript-prep`) | Taking clusters as truth; analyzing usability tests from the transcript alone |
| Quantitative | Suggest statistical tests, clean data, charts | Calculation errors, ignored statistical assumptions, confusing correlation with causation |
| Reporting | First draft, editing, adapting tone, summarizing | Personas/journeys "invented" without real data; hallucinations in chats over the repository (see `ux-insight-qa`) |

## 3. Main risks
- **Hallucination and fake sources** in desk research.
- **Analysis from the transcript alone**: participants do not verbalize micro-interactions or everything they see; they may misunderstand the product and the AI takes that as fact (NN/g 2023). Never analyze usability solely from what people say.
- **Vague, unprioritized summaries**: without study objectives and participant context, AI mixes trivial findings with important ones; reviewing that can take longer than analyzing by hand.
- **Missing quotes**: if it does not link each claim to session/timestamp you cannot verify; worse if it confuses your notes with what the participant said (always separate interpretation from evidence).
- **Bias**: "AI has no biases" is false. NN/g cites NIST: systemic, statistical/computational and human biases (e.g. anchoring when using its results). Dscout notes that much of the reference datasets come from the US (more than 60% according to a map cited in that article; 2022 data, treat as indicative).
- **Summaries that flatten**: minorities, sarcasm and emotional nuance are lost; automatic sentiment oversimplifies.
- **Imperfect anonymization**: one tool erased the names of the tools being evaluated, believing it protected privacy (NN/g example). Define rules for what to delete and spot-check.
- **Privacy/consent**: do not paste participant data into a public chatbot without consent and without anonymizing. It is hard to foresee the consequences of uploading sensitive data (Dscout).
- **Synthetic users**: they do not replace interviewing real people; a "persona" generated by ChatGPT is based on forums and blogs, not on your data (Godesky quote in Dscout).
- **Losing touch with the data**: several researchers prefer to analyze by hand because insights are born there (Dscout). Use AI after you have looked at the raw data.

## 4. Verification checklist (before using an AI output)
1. Did I ask for primary sources and open them?
2. Can every finding be traced to quote + session + timestamp?
3. Did I watch the usability videos myself (behavior), not only the transcript?
4. Did I check the transcript on noisy audio, accents or multiple speakers?
5. Did I rename/merge/split the clusters after going back to the raw data? (treat them as hypotheses)
6. Did I look for what is missing: minorities, edge cases?
7. Did I recalculate the figures and statistical assumptions?
8. Did I check consent, data permissions and PII?
9. Did an experienced researcher review questions and tasks for leading wording?
10. Did I mark in the report what was AI-assisted?

For a full audit of an analysis or report use `ux-insight-qa`.

## 5. Example prompts (adapted from NN/g and Looppanel)
- Ideation: ask for 15 possible questions for a diary study's daily survey, then have it pick and order the best 5-10 according to the objectives. A good starting point, not the final instrument.
- Documentation: upload the consent template and ask it to adapt it with the study details, asking first what information it is missing.
- Always ask: "follow research best practices and avoid leading questions"; if it fails, list the exact characteristics it must have.
- For ideation, first analyze a real existing output before generating new ideas (Looppanel).

## 7. Researching AI products (GitLab handbook guide)
1. Start from the user need, not the technology ("a technical solution looking for a problem"). With medium confidence: define the use case in JTBD format ("When [situation], I want [goal]") and validate with a survey (frequency and importance of the problem). With low confidence: generative research.
2. Validate before building with Wizard of Oz prototypes; include "wrong" answers because AI is probabilistic and this shows which errors people tolerate.
3. Do not ask "would you use it?": people predict their future behavior poorly; measure value and need.
4. Measure more than usability: a baseline of how they solve the problem today, trust, comfort giving feedback on a bad response, attitude toward third parties (e.g. the model provider).
5. Use at least three data points (three similar tasks), because AI output varies.
6. Learn the cost of errors: which are tolerable and which do harm.
7. Plan longitudinal research: mental models change with use. GitLab pilots an 8-construct survey (accuracy, reliability, value, control, error handling, guardrails, learning, AI limits) and a usability metric (effectiveness pass/fail, efficiency with the Single Ease Question, satisfaction). GitLab internal rule: if there is 1 failure, document it and replace the participant to reach 5; with 2 failures on the same task, stop, adjust and retry. This is an internal convention of that company, not a standard.

## 8. Contradictions and nuances
- NN/g 2023 evaluated AI tools and found them immature; their 2024 guide (revised in 2026) acknowledges improvements. Prioritize the newer version but keep the lesson about transcripts.
- Looppanel (a vendor) rates analysis as "tangible value" and NN/g asks for more caution: treat Looppanel as a commercial view and NN/g as the methodological reference.
- Tool vendors (e.g. Ween.ai according to Dscout) claim AI reduces human bias; NN/g qualifies this: it may reduce author confirmation bias but introduces other biases.
