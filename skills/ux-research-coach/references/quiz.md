# Quiz bank (with answers and the why)

Use: pick 5 questions, one at a time, mixing types. Correct each answer explaining the why before moving on. At the end, summarize topics to reinforce. If the learner answers wrong, do not give the answer at once: ask for a hint or their reasoning first. Questions are numbered 1-39; items marked (new) cover the newer skills.

## 1. Fundamentals
1. What does research do with data showing that nobody uses the "Share" function? A: data shows what happens, not why; you must find out whether they do not need it or cannot find it (Uxcel).
2. True or false: research makes the decisions for the team. A: false; it reduces risk and provides evidence, but it must be interpreted and turned into a decision.
3. A new feature has little usage. Is it always a design problem? A: no; shortly after launch it can be normal that it has not been discovered yet (interpretation context).
4. The three empathy questions: why do they want to achieve something? how can they achieve it? what happens next? Give an example of "what happens next" in a checkout. A: managing to pay but not knowing whether the order went through.
5. What is the discovery phase? A: researching and framing the problem space; no hypotheses or solutions are tested (Radiant).

## 2. Behavioral vs attitudinal
6. Classify: interview, heatmap, SUS, eye tracking, session recording, satisfaction survey, usability test. A: interview and survey = attitudinal; heatmap, eye tracking, recordings = behavioral; usability test combines both; SUS is a standardized questionnaire (quantitative attitudinal).
7. Why does eye tracking not explain why someone looked at an image for a long time? A: it shows what they looked at, not whether they liked it, were confused by it or rejected it.
8. Attitudinal data is more biased, why? A: subjectivity, memory, social desirability, and it changes depending on whether feedback is solicited or spontaneous.
9. An A/B test changed 12 things and conversion went up. What do you not know? A: which of the changes caused the effect.
10. Give an example of a behavioral method that is qualitative. A: watching individual session recordings.

## 3. Mental models and conventions
11. What does Jakob's Law say? A: users spend most of their time on other sites and expect yours to work the same way.
12. A team proposes an "innovative" navigation. What do you ask for? A: a user-benefit justification and a test with users; innovate in content, follow convention in structure.
13. "Pricing" vs "Investment": what is wrong and how do you verify it? A: internal jargon; card sorting/tree testing and interviews to use their language.
14. Mental model vs conceptual model. A: what the user believes vs what the designer wants to communicate; good design closes the gap.
15. Open or closed card sorting? A: open to discover categories; closed to validate a structure.

## 4. Contradictory data
16. The prototype is faster but users hate it. First four things to check. A: participants, tasks, logistics, analysis.
17. Why does measuring task time without success rate mislead? A: many studies count only successful attempts; it can be faster and fail more.
18. What is the initial productivity dip when something changes? A: learning a new interface is less efficient at first; hence learning studies and gradual rollout.

## 5. AI in research
19. Can an AI analyze a usability test from the transcript? A: no; people do not verbalize everything and behavior is the core.
20. You ask an AI for 10 test tasks. Main risk? A: leading tasks or tasks that give away the answer; review with an expert.
21. What do you give it so it drafts a consent form? A: a template and the study details; then review data permissions.
22. "This tool has no biases." True? A: false; there are systemic, statistical and human biases.
23. What do you do with the clusters an AI builds? A: treat them as hypotheses: rename, merge, split and go back to the raw data.
24. Why not ask "would you use this AI feature?" A: people predict their behavior poorly; measure need, value and trust.
25. When are AI-moderated interviews useful? A: structured feedback at scale, different languages, screening; not for complex or semi-structured topics.

## 6. Cases
26. Slack: why did small-change A/B tests not solve the problem? A: the problem was global complexity and hard to measure; it used co-design and benchmarking with novices.
27. Slack: what happened to the floating compose button on desktop? A: participants ignored it; it was moved above the sidebar.
28. Airbnb check-in: why is it not quantitative evidence? A: small cafe sample, the author's personal experience, no Airbnb data.
29. ClothezForU: which two methods would you add to the analysis? A: usability test and a behavior-triggered survey.

## 7. Newer skills
30. (new, usability testing) Why is "Do you like this page?" a bad task for a usability test? A: it asks for an opinion, not behavior; a good task is a realistic scenario with a goal ("find and book X") that does not name the interface labels, and you observe success, errors and time.
31. (new, usability testing) A participant says "this is easy" but took three attempts and backtracked twice. Which signal do you trust? A: the behavior; say-do gaps are common, so report success, errors and difficulty observed, and treat opinion as complementary. Keep the think-aloud going without leading ("what are you thinking now?").
32. (new, usability testing) How many participants for one qualitative round, and what is the caveat? A: about 5 per round/segment (NN/g reference); it finds the main problems but gives no reliable percentages, so for rates use a larger quantitative study (~20 or more) and report counts, not percentages, with small samples.
33. (new, transcript prep) Why pseudonymize a transcript before using an AI tool or sharing it with the team? A: names, employers and details can identify participants; replace them with codes, keep the key file separately and restricted, and spot-check because automatic anonymization misses or over-deletes (e.g. product names).
34. (new, transcript prep) What must be kept untouched when cleaning a transcript? A: the meaning and the participant's own words: remove filler only if it does not change meaning, keep timestamps and speaker labels, and never "fix" what someone said into something they did not say.
35. (new, insight QA) A report says "users hate the new checkout" with no quote or count. What do you do? A: ask for evidence: how many of how many participants, which sessions and timestamps, a verbatim quote; downgrade or rewrite the insight to what the data supports.
36. (new, insight QA) An insight is supported by one vivid quote. Is it solid? A: not by itself; a single quote is an anecdote. Check whether others show it, whether it was prompted by a leading question, and whether it matches behavior; label confidence (strong / moderate / weak).
37. (new, impact) How do you show that research mattered after the report? A: track the decisions it informed (what changed, who decided, when), the outcome measures chosen before (e.g. task success, support tickets), and the actions not taken; avoid claiming causation you cannot support.
38. (new, impact) A stakeholder says "we do not have time for research." What first move helps? A: tie a small, fast study to a specific pending decision and its cost of being wrong, using what evidence convinces that stakeholder (see the research profile); do not start with a big plan.
39. (new, onboarding) Why does `ux-research-onboarding` ask about decisions pending, constraints and what evidence convinces stakeholders before any method? A: the right method, depth and report format depend on context (stage, time, budget, privacy rules, audience); the answers go into `research-profile.md` so every other skill starts informed and does not re-ask, and it must never contain participant personal data.

## 8. Open exercises
- Write a research question for your product and decide whether it needs qualitative/quantitative and attitudinal/behavioral.
- Pick a label from your menu and propose how you would validate it.
- List 3 tasks in your flow that you would delegate to an AI and 3 that you would not, with the why.
- Take a finding from a past study and write the evidence trail behind it (quote, session, timestamp, count); say what is missing.
- Name one decision your last study informed and how you would know whether it worked.
