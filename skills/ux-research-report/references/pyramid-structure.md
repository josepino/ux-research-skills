# Pyramid structure (answer first)

## Contents
1. The idea · 2. "Purpose and method first" vs "answer first": how to reconcile them · 3. Skeleton · 4. Complete worked example · 5. When it does not fit

## 1. The idea
The Minto pyramid, which Looppanel proposes for research reports, starts with the conclusion and descends to the arguments and the evidence, so that someone who reads only the first screen still knows what to decide. Each level answers the question the level above provokes ("why?", "how do you know?").

## 2. The discrepancy and how to reconcile it
The classic report order opens with **purpose and methodology** (what we wanted to know and how). Pyramid-style guides (Looppanel, and others that recommend moving conclusions forward) open with **the answer**. They are compatible if the purpose enters as one line inside the first screen:
- **First page (pyramid):** purpose in one line -> answer/conclusion -> 3 findings -> recommendations and the decision requested -> method in one line ("8 interviews and 6 tests, September").
- **Body (classic order):** purpose and objectives, metrics, methodology, participants, findings, recommendations, appendices. Readers who need to judge rigor go down to here.
- **In a presentation to non-specialists:** meeting goal and conclusion first; method on a single slide. With researcher peers or skeptics of qualitative work, move the method up (it is the first thing they will want to evaluate).

## 3. Skeleton
1. **Answer** (1-2 sentences) to the question that started the study.
2. **Three arguments** (findings) that support it, each with "X of N".
3. **Evidence** per argument: quote, clip, figure, screenshot.
4. **Recommendations and decision requested**, prioritized.
5. **Limits:** what the results do not show.
6. **Method and sample** (detail) and appendices.
Test: reading only the headings of each level should tell the whole story.

## 4. Complete worked example (fictional data)
> **Study:** first-time purchase in the online store · Goal: understand why people abandon at payment.
>
> **Answer:** people abandon because they discover the shipping cost only on the last step. Showing it from the cart is the highest-impact, lowest-effort change.
>
> **Why we say so**
> 1. **The cost appears late and surprises people.** 5 of 8 interview participants mentioned it unprompted; 4 of 6 stalled at step 3 of the test. *"When I saw the total I thought: this isn't what they told me"* (P4, first-time buyer).
> 2. **After the surprise, they distrust the rest of the payment.** 3 of 8 hesitated to enter their card after seeing the surcharge. Single-source signal (interviews): not observed in the tests; to be cross-checked.
> 3. **Filters are not the main problem.** They were mentioned little, although 5 of 6 could not find them in the test: a real problem, second priority.
>
> **We recommend (by priority)**
> 1. Show the estimated shipping cost in the cart. High impact, low effort (to confirm with engineering).
> 2. Test a version with the total always visible at payment.
> 3. Move the filters to the listing header; repeat the test.
>
> **Decision we ask for:** prioritize recommendation 1 in the next cycle.
>
> **What we do not know:** whether it happens the same way on mobile (not tested), or how much of the measured abandonment this explains. Next study: mobile test with 5-6 people and a review of the payment-step analytics.
>
> **Method (one line):** 8 interviews and 6 moderated tests, September 2026, first-time buyers. Detail and appendices below.

## 5. When it does not fit
- Audiences that distrust the method and need to see rigor before accepting the conclusion.
- Surprising or politically delicate results: open with the context of the question so the answer does not read as an opinion.
- Record-keeping/audit reports: follow the organization's template and put the pyramid in the summary.
