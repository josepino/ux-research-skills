## 1. Chronological log vs notes by topic (NN/g)
| Situation | Method |
|---|---|
| Remote observers or in several places | Chronological log (digital) |
| Sequence matters for the analysis | Chronological log |
| Everyone in the same place, small study, you want quick consensus | Notes by topic (sticky notes, one observation per note) |
They can be combined. Typical log columns: participant (P1, no names), scenario/task, note, category, importance. Useful shortcuts: **V** = good moment for a video clip; **M** = methodology problem (e.g. a biased question). Mark your own ideas as such ("me:") so they do not mix with what was observed.
Notes by topic: one color per participant (not per note-taker), because you want to see at a glance how many users had the problem. Each note carries initials, scenario/task and an optional topic. Have one observer collect the notes and hand out the next color after each session.

## 2. Several observers (NN/g)
- Practical number: 4 to 10 note-takers per session (design, research, tech, stakeholders). Rotate some of them.
- Have each observer see at least 2 sessions, so they do not judge from a single person.
- Each takes notes separately; then they are combined. A shared live document with many people distracts, creates groupthink and dilutes responsibility.
- The simplest tool that works; less dependence on the network.

## 3. Frameworks to organize notes (Lyssna / Michele Ronsen)
- **Think-Feel-Say-Do**: four columns. Completed afterwards, from the transcript, not live. If a datum fits two, pick one or put it in both; do not overthink.
- **Opposites**: two contrasting columns (functionality vs aesthetics; simplicity vs feature richness).
- **Spectrum**: graduated levels (beginner to advanced; dissatisfied to delighted).
- The author's recommendation: apply at least 3 relevant frameworks to the same data.
- **Colors**: few, with a legend inside the file itself (positive, pain, neutral, repeated, outlier, key quote, follow-up). Without a legend you forget what orange was.
- Other frameworks for field notes (User Interviews): AEIOU (activities, environments, interactions, objects, users) and POEMS.
**Tension between sources:** NN/g proposes taking notes live (log or sticky notes); Ronsen advises against non-experts taking notes in real time and prefers coding afterwards from a transcript. Rule: live, only facts and quotes with shortcuts; interpretation and frameworks afterwards.

## 4. Facts vs interpretations
- Write what is observable ("opens the menu, sighs, frowns"), not the conclusion ("confused by the AI"). Interpretation goes in brackets or in a separate column (User Interviews).
- Opinions also slip into the debrief: record them, but mark which are observation and which are interpretation (Optimal Workshop).
- Noting the question asked also gives context to the answer. Grammar does not matter if you can understand yourself later.
- Depth by method: small usability tests, 1-2 pages of highlights; ethnography or deep conversation, 15-20 pages or more (User Interviews citing Zuzarte).

## 5. Debrief: time, preparation, moderation
- Typical duration 30 min to 1 h; the format matters less than **closeness to the session** (Uxcel). Up to 15 min is better than nothing.
- Calendar trick: if the interview lasts 60 min, schedule 90 and make the final 15-30 the debrief.
- Preparation (about 15 min): board with prompts (what stood out, quotes, surprises, open questions); tell observers what to note ("the participant's exact words"); block time in the invitation.
- Moderating: format rules, define terms ("pain" = persistent problem), strict timer (e.g. 5 min generate + 5 min discuss).
- A debrief is not a design meeting: first observations and interpretations, solutions to the parking lot. If people insist on jumping to solutions, maybe the study goals were not clear (Uxcel).
- Move from a superficial reaction to an insight ("didn't like the button" -> "fears losing their progress").
- Save the board: photo or export before anyone leaves.
- A study debrief with two separate stages (Games User Research): 1) share what was learned, 2) decide what to do (diverge, then converge). Be careful about including design recommendations in the report: they bias ideas and lack the production/art/tech view; better to pose challenges like "how might we…?".
- Base questions (Optimal Workshop): what stood out for you? what was surprising? which question was missing? Also ask for feedback on your moderation.

## 6. Top findings board (NN/g)
After each session: each observer says the most important thing; the facilitator writes it on a board (out of the participants' sight) with usability-oriented wording and a **tally** mark when a finding reappears. One single list for the whole study, not one per participant (that prevents counting recurrence). Photograph the board (it gets erased or notes fall off) and have someone transcribe it to a spreadsheet during the day. Advantage: in agile settings the preliminary result is out almost when the sessions end. Disadvantages: it is not searchable or sortable, and space runs out.
Silent variant (Uxcel): board sections, write without talking, post everything at once, and only then discuss and group. It gives quiet people equal weight.

## 7. Parking lot (NN/g)
It is a visible group artifact. Risk: being taken as a discard ("idea graveyard"). Five practices:
1. Dedicated time: the last 20-30 min of the meeting or a follow-up 1-2 days later.
2. Explain purpose and rules at the start; items are grayed out, time-boxed and then parked.
3. Organize as you go (affinity) and assign by cluster if time is short.
4. An owner and a date for every item (who does what, when).
5. Include it in the minutes.
Idea: a separate parking lot just for research questions/assumptions.

## 8. Debrief between interviews: 4 steps (LogRocket)
1. **Synthesize** into a snapshot of about a 1-minute read: name/photo or code, key facts, insights, opportunities, memorable quote, admin data (date, interviewer, links).
2. **Go deeper** (about 10 min of talk): how does it compare to previous interviews? a separate segment? anything contradict our beliefs?
3. **Update artifacts**: persona, journey map, etc.
4. **Decide**: continue as is; adjust (questions, participants, logistics); or stop (an insight that changes everything, diminishing returns through repetition, or ineffective interviews because of the wrong audience/questions).
The author's idea: better 10 interviews with debriefs than 15 rushed ones. Yux's questions: what went wrong? which new topics to dig into? which to drop as biased or obvious?

## 9. Field, remote, asynchronous
- Minimum: one debrief at the end of each day (Yux). Bold Insight: a daily debrief with the client and stakeholders to align, adjust the next sessions, sketch the report and avoid surprises; with time zones, it can go at the start of the day.
- Dispersed team: Yux ended up using WhatsApp (everyone has it, low connectivity, history, voice notes); fixed format: status/day/interview number + profile, then learnings by segment.
- Asynchronous (Uxcel): a channel already in use (Slack, a doc), initial analysis, **lead with surprises and contradictions**, direct questions tagging people, photos of the context, daily cadence, and schedule a live meeting as soon as possible. It does not replace the debrief.
- With participants: at the close, ask for questions and explain what was being tested; it works as a mini-interview to ask what could not be asked before (e.g. they ignored a button).
  Nuances (Ilioi, UX Collective, 2019; summarized text): debriefing participants is standard practice in psychology (mandatory if deception was used) and a complement to informed consent; in UX it is done after the task, so as not to bias it. Benefits noted: contrasting observations with the user's reasoning, not leaving them in the dark about what they spent their time on, empathy in both directions and a mini co-creation session. Caution: observation notes weigh more than what the participant says afterwards (self-protection bias); you do not need to reveal confidential information, part of the goal is enough.

## 10. Common mistakes
- Leaving the debrief "for later" or without reserved time.
- Jumping to solutions; not distinguishing observation from opinion.
- A parking lot without an owner or a date.
- Real names or files like "notes_final(3)"; colors without a legend.
- One findings list per participant; not photographing the board.
- A single observer who sees 1 session and draws conclusions.

## 11. Notes with AI (User Interviews, 2025)
Human + AI hybrid: AI transcribes and summarizes; the human captures the unsaid (hesitation, glances, atmosphere). Check that it is recording; AI can mishear or invent; it does not replace analysis. Using an AI summary as a trigger for the debrief reduces memory bias. Mark key moments live with tags. If there is personal data, check consent before uploading recordings to a tool (`ux-ethics-bias-review`).

## 12. Biases between collection and synthesis (Sam, UX Collective, 2019; summary)
The author calls the stretch between gathering data and synthesizing the "messy middle" (post-its per participant, memory, summaries). Since it only repackages data, it should not add its own judgment. Four common practices and their risk:
1. Noting what is "interesting" after each interview: it depends on the experience and culture of the note-taker; what seems "normal" is lost and the participant is poorly represented.
2. Relying on working memory: what is striking remains; audio is reviewed to verify quotes, rarely to check that the participant was captured completely.
3. Quick summaries to bring people on board: richness drops at every handoff (the author's illustrative figures, 100% -> 70% -> 40%; they are not measured data).
4. Taking individual notes during the oral presentation of the summary: group pressure, many repeated notes and the illusion that more post-its = more severity. Those notes should not pass into synthesis.
The author offers no solution. Practical mitigation compatible with this skill: note facts and quotes during the session (not only the "interesting"), contrast the debrief with the recording or transcript to see which participant is underrepresented, and do not use the number of notes as a measure of severity (use recurrence per participant, the tally from section 6).

## 13. Facebook debrief formats (O'Neill, UX Collective, 2018; summary, text truncated at the end)
Purposes: learn (what mattered to each observer, misunderstandings), build consensus (nobody feels their prior idea "loses") and prioritize (what is actionable now vs what is big).
- **Moderated conversation**, as soon as possible (even over dinner). Move from the superficial ("didn't like the button") to the actionable ("they feared leaving the site"): explore what happened and what it could mean. Clear ground rules, set decisions aside and take the moderator's own notes (they show what matters to stakeholders).
- **Joint whiteboard**, variants: post-its with prompts and reorder by severity or ease of fix; group by theme; rank problems; a sheet per participant on the wall that is filled in during each session; a sheet per participant "popcorn style"; divide and conquer (one note-taker per participant, share at the end of the day and a sheet of common themes).
- **Asynchronous**: share while the study is still running, with daily cadence or space between participants; post surprises and contradictions rather than verbatim quotes; tag people with questions. Warning: it is more broadcast than dialogue and does not replace face-to-face conversation.
- **Traffic light** (J. Shuang): each person sorts individually into green (keep going), red (stop/fix) and yellow (to define/questions). **Top 5** (L. Roth Rivera): top 5 insights, top 5 next steps and an impact/effort matrix; well received by cross-functional teams. There is also a "mini-debrief" after each session plus a big one at the end (description cut off in the source).
**Tension with section 6:** the sheet/page per participant gives traceability of who said what, but clashes with NN/g's recommendation of a single list with a tally to count recurrence. Rule: a sheet per participant during the session (input) and a single findings list at the debrief.

## 14. Facilitated asynchronous debrief (educators' manual, Centennial College, CC BY-NC 4.0; adaptation)
A source from another field (simulation in nursing education); use only as an analogy. Elements that transfer to a remote team debrief or one with participants in a forum:
- A trained facilitator (debriefing is a skill that is learned) who knows the material well.
- A confidentiality statement: who sees the forum and whether it can be downloaded.
- Repeat the goal, the estimated time, how many questions there are and how many contributions are expected; a few well-chosen questions, not an avalanche.
- A window of at least 48 h to reflect (a recommendation from the education domain; in a product team less usually suffices, but it serves as a reference).
- Advantage: it starts immediately, with fresh ideas. Disadvantage: the thread fragments when each person posts at a different time; the facilitator must step in to steer and clarify wrong assumptions.
- Provide a contact for technical support.

## 15. Notes: why take them if there is a recording (Kumar, UX Planet, 2021; summary)
- A recording or transcript is costly to reopen (the author estimates about 2,500 words per 15 min of audio, an approximate figure); notes guide what to re-ask, which topics were covered and where to return.
- Get familiar with the topic and carry a guide with blank spaces between questions; for observation use AEIOU or POEMS (already in section 3: POEMS = people, objects, environments, messages, services).
- With several observers, individual notes and then combine (matches section 2).
- Complete the notes within 10 min afterwards, so anyone can understand them, and separate verbatim quotes from observations.
- Storage: naming convention and folders from the start (the author's example: topic / moderator_participant_date / note-taker_file). See `repository-and-naming.md` section 9.
