# Lean UX for the research plan

When to read this file: the person has a new idea/product, little time or budget, or needs to turn team assumptions into testable hypotheses.

## Core idea
Lean UX prioritizes outcomes over deliverables: instead of asking "what are we going to design", it asks "why" and what we want to achieve. A hypothesis is formulated before building. The term is associated with Janice Fraser (adapting UX to Lean Startups); the reference book is Jeff Gothelf's. See sources.md.

## Think - Make - Check cycle
| Phase | What is done | Example activities |
|---|---|---|
| Think | Expected outcomes, assumptions, research, ideation | competitor analysis, stakeholder interviews, team ideation, storyboards, value proposition |
| Make | Build the minimum to test the hypothesis (MVP) | sketch, wireframe, prototype, landing page |
| Check | Validate with users and data | usability test, A/B, analytics, user and stakeholder feedback |
With what was learned, go back to "Think". Reducing cycle time matters more than building fast (Fraser, 2011).

## Assumptions -> hypotheses
1. Write assumptions on four fronts: business outcome, users, user outcome (the pain we solve) and functionality.
2. Turn each risky assumption into a hypothesis. Formula: "We believe that [doing this] for [these users] will achieve [this outcome]. We will know when [measurable signal]."
3. Example (adapted from Adam Fard): assumption "conversion is low because the main button is not visible" -> hypothesis "we believe a bigger button, identified by new customers, will raise conversion by X%". The number X is defined by the team, not by the skill.
4. A hypothesis is tested, not debated: define the success/failure criterion BEFORE looking at results.
5. Prioritize assumptions with the most risk and least evidence; those go into the plan first.

## The MVP must be able to test the hypothesis
- Fidelity proportional to what is tested: a sign-up form is tested with a wireframe; a button microinteraction needs a high-fidelity prototype with color and content (UXPin).
- Cheap options: paper for early ideas, clickable prototype, a landing page that measures interest (emails, pre-sales) without having a product.

## Methods that fit a lean environment (Adam Fard)
User interviews, usability testing, guerrilla testing (less structured), A/B (only with enough sample). A lean researcher prioritizes speed and immediate actionability; one without pressure can do broader, more methodical studies. The research goals are the same.

## Useful principles for the team (summary of UXPin, 15 in total)
Small multidisciplinary teams (UXPin cites 5-9 people), focus on problems rather than features, continuous discovery, "get out of the building" (test with users instead of debating internally), externalize the work (show sketches), do rather than over-analyze, minimal documentation, permission to fail.
Caveat: UXPin cites the phrase "move fast and break things" as a principle; in research with people, speed never justifies skipping consent or privacy (`ux-ethics-bias-review`).

## Measuring the success of a lean cycle (MentorMate)
- Qualitative: continuous validation with real users, or with sales and support people who know their behavior.
- Quantitative: choose KPIs at the start to have a baseline. Cited examples: time on task, error rate, search use versus navigation.

## Common mistakes
- Calling "lean" not doing research. Lean reduces waste, not evidence.
- Hypotheses without a measurable signal or threshold.
- An MVP that cannot refute the hypothesis.
- Debating in meetings what can be tested in hours. Fraser illustrates the waste: a design decision that costs months can be found wrong in hours with a test.
- Going back to waterfall "under another name": rigid phases and heavy deliverables.

## Lean vs Agile
They are not mutually exclusive: most lean teams are agile. Lean focuses on reaching the MVP quickly and validating; Agile on the iterative process, with or without an MVP (Adam Fard, UXPin). Note: the sources contradict each other on the origin of Lean UX ("derived from Toyota" vs "derived from the 2003 book Lean Software Development"); this is history and does not change practical use.
