# Research roadmap, prioritization and strategy

When to read this file: there are several study requests, the person wants a quarterly/annual roadmap, must say "no" to a request, or asks when to research in the product cycle.

## Strategy vs. plan vs. roadmap
- **Plan** = one study. **Roadmap** = several studies over time. Research **strategy** = what kind of research the team does, for whom, with what limits. Lyssna distinguishes research strategy (a subset) from UX strategy (the global vision of the product).
- User Interviews proposes four questions: who is the research for? what will be researched? when? how does it support the business? They also work for a one-person team.

## When to research
- Discovery: understand pains and mental models, validate personas, identify desired features, benchmark. Typical methods: stakeholder and user interviews, observation, product audit, experience maps (Openfield).
- Design/prototype: usability testing, flows, interaction validation, content comprehension, brand/UI testing (Openfield).
- Post-launch: analytics, surveys, new rounds. Think of it as continuous research, not a one-off study.
- Frequent mistake: waiting for the engineering phase to talk to users; afterwards, rework interrupts the product roadmap.

## "Now / later / further out" roadmap (UXlib, Cribbs and Daugherty 2024)
1. **Understand your capacity**: time, system maturity, stakeholders' attitude toward change, type of product, approvals (e.g. ethics committee), funds for incentives, assistants and tools.
2. **Articulate organizational alignment** with a "guiding phrase" that cites the organization's mission/vision or strategic plans.
3. **Choose 2 methods or activities** according to your readiness and define the project as: finding problems/opportunities, validating ideas, or evaluating design/performance.
4. **Worksheet (4 stretches with start/end dates)**: method (what), objective (why), procedure (how), timeline (when), participants (who), materials, data management plan; consider external factors that lower validity (bias, improvised application).
5. Guiding questions of the worksheet: who is the audience and what demographics? what is their biggest frustration? how will you share the roadmap and receive internal feedback? how will you communicate the user perspective during development?
6. Later phases it contemplates: prototyping, feedback channels, communication plan, internal training, evaluating ROI and value, disseminating findings.
Note: the template is of academic/library origin; adapt it to product language.

## Quarterly roadmapping process (UXinsight, Backbase)
1. Regular cycle (they use quarterly).
2. Stakeholder workshop: high-level goals, questions/hypotheses, decisions to make, product stage, relative size (a quick unmoderated test vs. a contextual inquiry in several countries).
3. Prioritization criteria (weight them in a scored matrix):
   - Team's willingness to change its mind (learn or "validate" its idea?).
   - Clarity about what will be done with the insights.
   - Alignment among stakeholders on the goals.
   - Cost/value ratio.
   - Timing: enough lead time but not so far away that priorities change.
   - Balance of exploratory vs. evaluative.
4. Fill the roadmap with the highest scores; deprioritize the lowest.
5. **Warning signs**: the team did not act on the previous study; low commitment (slow responses); they ask to research without doing secondary research first; no time/resources to act on results; extreme deadlines. Response: proceed with caution making the risk explicit, or deprioritize.
6. Make the criteria explicit and try to own the priorities.

## Scope, speed and rigor (User Interviews)
- Decide what NOT to research; a clear criterion lets you say "no".
- Factors cited for choosing a method: the question (87%) and time (74%) (State of User Research 2025). Median over six months: 2 mixed studies, 3 qualitative and 1 quantitative.
- Define when a quick "pulse check" is enough and when to defend a study with sample and rigor.
- Align with metrics that matter to the business (NPS, recurring revenue) and record the impact.

## Questions before the strategy (Lyssna)
The guide proposes 7 questions before planning; the first three were confirmed: what are the main goals? who is the audience? what resources (time, budget, people) do you have? If resources are missing, prioritize activities.

## Sample size when planning (NN/g)
- Qualitative usability study: 5 users per study; many small rounds are better than one big one.
- Several very different user groups: 3-4 per group.
- Quantitative: at least 20; card sorting: at least 15 per group.
- With very low-cost agile processes, 2 users per study can be optimal; in other projects, 8 or more.
- What is not fixed now is fixed in the next iteration: plan for iterations.

## Pitfalls when structuring a program (Openfield)
Starting late; confusing research with testing; underfunding; overspending on a single big test; not researching often enough.

## Annual roadmap of a research team (IBM Design, Pekker 2021; blog source, partial text)
Complements the quarterly process above: it is an annual plan for the team, not for a single study. Use it when joining a team/project or every year.
1. **Understand stakeholder needs** with interviews (design, PM, devs, writing) and review the product roadmap to align milestones with generative studies (opening projects/opportunities) or evaluative ones (usability).
2. **Assess the current state**: what research exists (personas, journeys, need statements), what is outdated, what is known and what is not; talk to previous researchers, designers and PMs to avoid redundant studies.
3. **High-level research goals** (outcomes, not studies; e.g. "validate our personas"), including ResearchOps (best practices, enabling data-driven design).
4. **Plan studies**: tactical (tied to the product roadmap, simple and short, e.g. a test after implementing a design) vs strategic (long-term goals, such as the full journey; they combine methodologies and sometimes several researchers). Table with goals in columns and studies in rows, marking which goal each study supports (almost all support more than one).
5. **Timeline**: tactical first (they follow the product; design estimates when prototypes will exist), strategic with more flexibility. Reference from the source: at most 1-2 strategic plus 1 tactical at a time; distribute by quarter and then a monthly Gantt.
6. **Share with stakeholders**: highlight the main gaps and how they will be covered, so they know you listened and stay involved.
When maintaining it: it belongs to the team, it is not your task list; make the tactical part flexible and communicate changes (mid-year review), but make sure last-minute requests do not divert the strategic goals.
Contrast with the above: UXinsight prioritizes with a criteria matrix and a quarterly cycle; IBM proposes annual with a mid-year review. The approaches are compatible (choose according to how fast the product's priorities turn over).
