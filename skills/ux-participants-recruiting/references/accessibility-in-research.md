# Accessible and inclusive research: operating guide

Contents: 1. Before starting · 2. Two ways to include · 3. Timeline and budget · 4. Recruiting · 5. Prototype fidelity · 6. Examples by stage · 7. Location · 8. Materials · 9. Pilot · 10. Language and treatment · 11. What to avoid

## 1. Before starting (Dscout, Grellmann)
- Accessibility is not a one-time activity: every change in content or structure can break it. Before investing in the research, secure the team's commitment to fix what turns up.
- When evaluating a live or soon-to-launch product, **run an accessibility audit first** (without users, against WCAG 2.1 AA): it prevents participants from getting stuck at the first interaction and the session being wasted.
- For early-stage products, include people with disabilities in generative research: they influence the value proposition, not just accessibility.

## 2. Two ways to include
1. In every stage, a part of the sample with accessibility needs (e.g. 2 of 8). Preferred by Dscout.
2. In selected stages, the whole sample with accessibility needs; useful when there is legal/compliance pressure before launch.
If you can only include one person, do it anyway: "accessibility is a journey".

## 3. Timeline and budget
- Ask for more time before, during and after. Dscout example: an agency that takes 2 weeks may ask for 3; instead of 6 one-hour sessions a day, 3 of 90 minutes; between sessions, 30 minutes instead of 15.
- Expect fatigue (one participant talked less and less as she tired); include breaks.
- Bigger budget: agency fees and higher incentives, travel by the person's preferred means, assistive technology licenses if it is a lab (better a lab that already has them), and an easy reimbursement process.

## 4. Recruiting
- The more criteria, the harder it is: relax the others (e.g. bank: "account opened in the last 3 months" → 6 or 12 months).
- If you recruit yourself: disability organizations and associations, forums, communities, and customers who have already written about accessibility. Build an ongoing relationship.
- Beware of local regulations that limit asking about disability; be transparent in the notice that you are looking for participants for accessibility research.
- Prioritize **accessibility needs and ways of using** over diagnoses: nobody fits a category and having several is common.
- Cover variety: visual (color blindness, low vision, blindness), physical, cognitive (including learning/neurological), hearing, speech; and within each, severity, experience with the technology and brand (JAWS, NVDA, TalkBack, VoiceOver).
- Example specification for pre-launch evaluation (Dscout): 3 screen reader users (1 JAWS, 1 VoiceOver, 1 NVDA) and 2 magnification users (1 Zoom, 1 ZoomText).
- A Dscout screener proposes asking "Would you describe yourself as a person with a disability? No / Not sure / Yes / (write in)" when the ability is relevant.

## 5. Prototype fidelity
Assistive technology needs programmatically available content. Rule (Dscout): if the prototype is functional HTML, you can recruit assistive technology users; if it is paper, wireframe or a prototype without functionality, you cannot. Advanced alternative: the moderator imitates the screen reader (Wizard of Oz), only with a very experienced moderator. Do not assume your prototyping tool is accessible (screen reader support in some design tools, Figma among them, has been reported as limited).

## 6. Examples by stage (Dscout)
- Generative/discovery at home: wide variety; loose criteria; prioritize those who would not be able to travel later.
- Agile iterations: variety across rounds, not within each one; adjust the profile to how functional the prototype is.
- Pre-launch lab evaluation: specific representation per technology (see the example).

## 7. Location
- **Person's home/place**: usually gives better results (relaxed, their devices and settings, you see needs beyond the screen). Bring a second camera to record the assistive technology.
- **Remote**: verify that *all* the video call software is accessible (sign-up, settings, screen sharing); send instructions and do a test call; guide screen reader users so the reader's audio reaches the microphone/recording; consider live captions.
- **Lab**: visit it beforehand. Step-free access at doors, restrooms and emergency exits; space for a guide dog, wheelchair and a companion or interpreter; height-adjustable desks and varied chairs; avoid soft carpets; adjustable lighting. Do not ask a JAWS user to use NVDA: they are different products; match the brand and, if you can, the version. If they bring their own equipment, have a way to record it.

## 8. Materials
Send consent and information sheet **beforehand**, in the format they prefer (ask): it avoids time pressure when signing. Large print and simple language. Not all blind people use braille; with a screen reader, send an accessible digital format. PDFs can be inaccessible: prefer text in the email, an online form, audio or video. NN/g accessibility reports (a paid 47-page report) recommend in-person sessions with the person's usual technology and cover consent, recording and sample screeners (only the public summary was read).

## 9. Pilot
Over-recruit and include someone with accessibility needs in the pilot: a colleague without them does not prepare you in the same way. Check known barriers in the prototype, timing, and that the recording captures everything. If the pilot goes well, its data can go into the analysis.

## 10. Language and treatment (Dscout inclusive research guide)
- Gender: man / woman / non-binary / prefer to self-identify (not just sex); allow self-description and multiple selection for identity; avoid "Other" (prefer "Prefer to self-identify" or "Add my own").
- Person-first language ("person with paraplegia"), though it varies by region and community; when in doubt, let the person define themselves. Some deaf people consider deafness a matter of language, not disability.
- **Recruit by behavior, not identity**: if you study men's trousers shopping, recruit those who buy them, not those who identify as men. Do not automatically exclude by identity.
- Avoid idioms, jargon, acronyms and metaphors; the burden of understanding is on the researcher.
- Check affinity bias (we tend to choose people like us) by reviewing the demographic composition before closing the selection.
- Topics that may retraumatize: warn from the screener, allow skipping questions.
- Pre-session message to request reasonable adjustments: introduce yourself, state the date and ask what adaptations they need.
- Research tools (screeners, platforms) must also be accessible (contrast, keyboard navigation).

## 11. What to avoid
Assuming "5 users" is enough to cover diverse technologies; testing with only one screen reader; sessions of 6 a day; untested PDF forms; choosing the disability label instead of the concrete need.
