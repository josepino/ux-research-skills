# Scored screener

Complements `screener-template.md` (wording and funnel). This page covers the cases where there are more candidates than slots, or where you suspect tailor-made answers to collect the incentive.

## 1. Must-haves vs preferences
- **Must-have** (hard filter): without it the person cannot give useful data for the research question (uses the product, role, market, availability). Failing it disqualifies. Keep few: every hard filter shrinks the pool and lengthens the timeline.
- **Preferred** (adds points): improves fit but is not indispensable (high frequency, a certain device, previous experience).
- **Quota**: balances the final group (segments, devices, assistive technology). Applied after ranking.
- **Exclusion**: works on the product, the competition or research; took part recently in another of our studies.
Test the rule: if a person passes the must-haves and fails every preference, are they still useful? If the answer is no, that preference is really a must-have.

## 2. Scoring to rank candidates
The score **ranks**, it does not decide: it helps choose among those who already passed the hard filters. The weights are our own judgment (heuristic, not a published standard); define them before seeing the answers so you do not tune them to the candidates.
```
Criterion                                Points
Product use in the last month            0 / 1 / 2
Relevant usage scenario                  0 / 1 / 2
Required device or context               0 / 1
Specific open answer (see section 3)     0 / 1 / 2
Availability in the window               0 / 1
```
- Rules table: question → answer → points / disqualify / quota. Keep it next to the screener.
- Select by descending score while respecting quotas; the next ones become backups.
- Review borderline cases by hand. A low score for belonging to a rare segment may be exactly what you want: the quota wins.
- Do not use the score to exclude on demographics that are not a criterion of the research question.

## 3. Attention questions and red flags
- **Attention question**: a simple instruction hidden in the grid ("for this row select 'Almost never'"). Whoever fails it is probably answering without reading. Only one per screener, and do not punish a single mistake on small screens.
- **Specificity open question**: "tell me about the last time you {activity}". Generic, copied or off-topic answers are a signal; specific ones (details, tools, context) score.
- **Consistency**: the same information asked in two different ways should not contradict itself.
- **Time**: finishing in seconds something that takes minutes is a clue, not proof.

## 4. "Professional" participants and incentive self-selection
Signals (all are clues, none is proof): they accept every option in a list, say they meet all criteria, answers copied across candidates, emails with repeated patterns, recent participation in our studies, they know "what you are looking for".
What to do:
1. Do not reveal the criterion or the detailed topic in the invitation; use a broad description.
2. Include credible distractors and the "none of the above" option.
3. Verify the essentials when confirming (a short call or a reconfirmation question).
4. Keep a participation log per person and define a waiting period between studies (team policy, you decide it).
5. If you detect a poor-quality session, note it in the panel (`panel-hygiene.md`) without accusing. Apply the same incentive out of respect, unless an explicit policy says otherwise.
6. Avoid over-punishing: a false positive discards a real person.

## 5. Tests before launch
Try it with 2-3 people; check that the rules disqualify who they should, that no valid combination is left without a slot, and that the logic works on mobile. Write the rules down before receiving answers.
